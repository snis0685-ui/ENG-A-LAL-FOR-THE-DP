01 Advertisement (print / commercial) The stimulus is a product, service or campaign image with a slogan and body copy.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Built on AIDA — Attention (image/headline), Interest (copy), Desire (benefit), Action (CTA)
  • Visual hierarchy: one dominant image, a headline, a strapline, then small print
  • The call-to-action is placed last and made unmissable
Language & diction
  • Imperatives drive the CTA — 'Discover', 'Own', 'Belong'
  • Second-person 'you/your' builds a one-to-one relationship
  • Superlatives and neologisms manufacture uniqueness ('unmissable', 'redefined')
  • Connotation over denotation — every noun sells a feeling, not a fact
Persuasive appeals
  • Ethos — brand logo, heritage, celebrity endorsement
  • Pathos — aspiration, belonging, fear of missing out
  • Logos — spec, price-point, 'clinically proven', statistics
Visual & layout
  • Colour psychology carries the brand's promise (calm blues, urgent reds)
  • Anchorage — the caption/slogan fixes an otherwise open image
  • Negative space signals premium; clutter signals value/urgency
  • Model's gaze directs the viewer (demand) or the product (offer)
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Name the appeal AND its effect on the target demographic
  • Read the image and the copy as one argument, not two features
  • Notice what is absent — no price often signals a luxury positioning
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

A full-page magazine advertisement for a fictional watch brand, MERIDIAN — a single monochrome image, a headline, and a short block of body copy.

“YOU DON’T FIND TIME. YOU MAKE IT.” The headline runs the width of the page in stark capitals, and beneath it a man in his forties stands alone on an empty railway platform at dawn, coat open, unhurried while the world blurs past him. The watch is barely visible on his wrist — small, almost withheld, because the advertisement is not selling a watch. It is selling the kind of man who owns one. The body copy is three short sentences: “Some people wait for the train. Some people are the reason it runs on time. Which one are you?” That closing rhetorical question is the whole strategy — it does not describe the product, it dares the reader to place themselves on the flattering side of a division the advertiser has just invented. Notice the diction of control and scarcity: “make”, “reason”, “on time” — a vocabulary of mastery that flatters an aspirational professional audience. The palette is deliberate: cold greys and a single warm glint of amber off the watch face, so the eye is led, almost against its will, to the only point of colour on the page — and to the logo directly beneath it. Ethos is carried by the brand’s serif wordmark and the tagline “Established 1911”, borrowing the authority of heritage; pathos by the fantasy of composure the image sells; and the only concession to logos is a single line of six-point type at the foot of the page, giving the movement and water resistance almost apologetically. The man never looks at the camera — an “offer” image that invites the reader to imagine themselves into his stride rather than be addressed by it. There is no price anywhere on the page, and that absence is itself an argument: if you have to ask, this object was never meant for you.

02 Appeal (charity / fundraising) A plea for donation, action or awareness from an NGO or cause.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Shock-then-hope arc: confront the problem, then hand the reader agency
  • One named individual carries the whole cause — a face, not a statistic
  • Ends on a concrete, costed ask and a frictionless way to give
Language & diction
  • Emotive, sensory diction humanising the sufferer
  • Inclusive 'we' plus empowering 'you can'
  • Present tense for immediacy: 'Right now, a child is...'
  • Concrete equivalence — 'just 3 a month feeds a family'
Persuasive appeals
  • Pathos dominates, but statistics arrive for credibility
  • Guilt balanced with empowerment so the reader feels able, not helpless
  • Moral obligation framed as an easy, immediate choice
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Track the pivot from despair to agency — that turn is the design
  • Note the photograph's direct eye-contact (a 'demand' image)
  • The specific sum makes an abstract crisis feel solvable
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

A direct-mail fundraising letter from a fictional clean-water charity, WELLSPRING — a photograph, a headline, and a costed ask with a tear-off donation slip.

“Amara is six years old. She has walked four miles for water she wouldn’t let you wash your hands in.” The headline sits beneath a photograph in which the child looks directly out of the page, straight into the reader’s eyes — a “demand” image that makes refusal feel personal. The first paragraph is deliberately unbearable: it describes the colour of the water, the walk before sunrise, the younger brother who did not survive last year’s cholera. Every detail is concrete and sensory rather than statistical, because a number cannot be pitied and a named child can. Then, at the fold, the letter turns. Where the opening withholds hope, the second half hands the reader agency: “You can change Amara’s story before you finish your coffee this morning.” The pivot from despair to empowerment is the entire architecture of the appeal — guilt opens the wound, and the donation is offered as the way to close it. The ask is made frictionless and specific: “Just £3 a month — the price of that coffee — gives a family clean water for life.” That equivalence shrinks an overwhelming global crisis to a sum the reader already spends without thinking, converting helplessness into a manageable, almost effortless act of goodness. The verbs turn imperative toward the close — “Give today. Post the slip tonight. Don’t let another dawn break over that walk.” — and the inclusive register (“Together, we can…”) folds the donor into the charity’s own moral community. Statistics do appear, but late and sparingly: “1 in 10 people on earth has no safe water” arrives only after the reader already cares about one of them. The postscript — the most-read line in any letter — repeats the ask in miniature: “P.S. Amara is waiting. £3 is all it takes.”

03 Autobiography / Memoir extract First-person account of the writer's own remembered life.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Selective, thematic memory — not a full chronology
  • A frame: the remembered scene plus the reflection it prompts now
  • Zooms between a single vivid moment and its lifelong meaning
Voice & perspective
  • Dual perspective — the experiencing-I (then) and the narrating-I (now)
  • Retrospective wisdom colours, but doesn't erase, the younger self
  • Confessional honesty, including self-criticism
Language & diction
  • Novelistic sensory detail reconstructs a scene
  • Figurative language carries emotional truth
  • Register shifts with the age being remembered
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Separate the two 'I's — where does the older voice intrude to judge?
  • The insight ('why this memory matters') is the real subject
  • Look for the small, specific object that unlocks the whole feeling
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

An extract from a memoir in which the adult narrator remembers the afternoon, aged nine, that she understood her father was afraid of something.

I was nine the first time I saw my father frightened, though it would take me thirty years to give the expression its right name. The dual voice is there in the opening sentence itself — the child who merely saw, and the adult who now, from the far side of a life, can interpret. That afternoon he was mending the fence, and a car slowed on the road behind us and did not pass. I remember the smell of creosote and cut grass, the exact weight of the hammer he handed me “to hold” — the sensory precision of memoir, reconstructing a scene the way memory never actually preserves it, in high, novelistic definition. What I saw was a man who had simply stopped moving. What I understand now is that stillness is what fear looks like on a person who has decided, for the sake of a watching child, not to run. He said, “Go inside and put the kettle on, there’s a good girl,” and I went, obediently, mistaking dismissal for ordinary errand — and there is the gap the whole memoir lives inside: what the experiencing child felt, and what the narrating adult can finally see. The car eventually moved on. Nothing, in the language of plot, happened. But everything happened, because that was the afternoon I learned that my father was a person the world could threaten, and that his gentleness with me had a cost I would not be asked to pay until much later. Memory is not a record; it is a negotiation, and I have negotiated that hour so many times that I can no longer swear the car was blue. What I can swear to is the hammer, and the good-girl, and the terrible, protective calm of a man deciding not to let his child see him run. That, in the end, is the only fact that mattered.

04 Biography Third-person account of another person's life.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Chronological or thematic organisation of a life
  • Contextualises the subject within their era and milieu
  • Moves from evidence to an evaluation of significance
Language & diction
  • Third person, researched, evidence-anchored
  • Balances documented fact with interpretation of character
  • Reporting verbs signal the biographer's stance ('claimed' vs 'revealed')
Voice & stance
  • An evaluative narrator — sympathetic, critical or ambivalent
  • Quotation from letters, interviews and records lends authority
  • Judgement of legacy frames the whole
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Watch the reporting verbs — they leak the biographer's verdict
  • Interpretation vs fact: where does the writer editorialise?
  • Note whose sources are trusted and whose are doubted
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

An extract from a literary biography of the (fictional) composer Elsa Varga, covering the year her reputation collapsed.

By the winter of 1937, Varga had become, in the words of one Vienna critic, “a scandal set to music” — and the phrase, endlessly repeated, would outlive every note she wrote. The biographer’s hand is visible in that framing: a life is being organised not as a sequence of days but around a single interpretive claim, the year everything turned. The evidence is marshalled carefully. Her own letters, quoted at length, show a woman “too tired to be frightened”; her publisher’s ledgers, by contrast, record a commercial panic that the letters never mention — and the biography sets the two sources against each other, trusting the ledger over the letter, reading her composure as performance rather than fact. Notice the reporting verbs, which quietly carry the verdict: Varga “insisted”, her rival “conceded”, the board “claimed” — a vocabulary that grants credibility to some voices and withholds it from others without the biographer ever stating a preference outright. The wider context is drawn in with a novelist’s economy: the tightening politics of the city, the shrinking audiences, the way a private falling-out became, in that atmosphere, a public danger. The biographer resists the temptation to make her a simple victim. Varga is shown to have been proud, occasionally cruel to those who loved her, and disastrously certain that talent would protect her when it protected no one. The chapter closes not on the collapse but on its meaning: “She had spent her genius as though it were inexhaustible, and discovered, in a single season, the exact size of her fortune.” That final sentence is interpretation dressed as narration — the biographer’s evaluation of a whole character, delivered as if it were merely the next event. Fact and judgement, here, have become impossible to separate, which is precisely the biographer’s art.

05 Blog post Informal online article with a personal, conversational voice.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Short, scannable paragraphs under bold subheadings
  • Hooky opening line; a clear takeaway; a CTA to comment/share
  • Hyperlinks and embedded media woven through the body
Tone, register & voice
  • Conversational, contraction-rich, addressing 'you' directly
  • A strong, consistent persona — the reason people return
  • Opinion foregrounded over neutral reporting; humour and asides welcome
Language & diction
  • Colloquialism, rhetorical questions, direct address
  • Fragmented sentences for emphasis and pace
  • Jargon or in-group references signalling a niche community
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Name the persona and how diction constructs it
  • Note the platform features implied (comments, tags, share)
  • Register is the point — measure its distance from formal prose
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

A personal-finance blog post, chatty and opinionated, built to be skimmed on a phone.

Let’s be honest: your budgeting app is lying to you, and it’s doing it in a really soothing font. That’s the whole opening — a provocation, a contraction, and direct address, all in one breath, because online you have about four seconds before the reader’s thumb keeps scrolling. I spent two years colour-coding spreadsheets. Two. Years. (You’re allowed to judge me. I’m judging me.) And here’s the thing nobody selling you a “money mindset” wants to admit — the apps aren’t built to make you richer. They’re built to make you feel busy. The post is engineered for the skim-reader who will never read it properly: the subheadings do the real work — “The £4 Latte Myth,” “What Actually Moved My Needle,” “The Boring Thing That Worked” — so that even a five-second scan delivers the argument. Paragraphs run two lines, maximum. The voice is a friend who happens to have figured something out, not an expert on a stage: “I tried this so you don’t have to (you’re welcome).” That cultivated persona — the cheerfully cynical everyman — is the actual product being sold here, more than any tip. There are hyperlinks buried in the body (“I broke down the maths here”) that quietly do the platform’s bidding: keep you clicking, keep you on the site. Opinion is foregrounded and never apologised for — “Sinking funds are the only budgeting idea that has ever survived contact with my actual life” — because neutrality doesn’t get shared and certainty does. And it ends exactly where every post like this ends, with a call to action disguised as a conversation: “Tell me I’m wrong in the comments. Actually — tell me the one subscription you should cancel and won’t. I’ll go first.” Engagement, framed as intimacy.

06 Brochure / Leaflet Folded promotional or informational print piece.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Panelled layout that unfolds a deliberate sequence
  • Cover panel hooks; inner panels inform; back panel closes with contact/QR
  • Bite-sized chunks under bold subheadings
Language & diction
  • Benefit-led language — 'what you get', not 'what it is'
  • Bullet points, pull-quotes and icons for scannability
  • Second person and gentle imperatives
Visual & layout
  • Consistent brand colour, typography and imagery
  • Image-text pairing on each panel
  • Directional cues guide the fold-by-fold read
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Map the reading path the folds impose
  • Note the benefit-vs-feature framing of the copy
  • The back panel's function is conversion — find the ask
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

A three-fold brochure for a fictional heritage railway, THE VALE LINE — described panel by panel as the reader would unfold it.

The cover panel gives you almost nothing, and that is the point: a single sepia photograph of a steam engine emerging from mist, and four words — “Step Back In Time.” An imperative and a promise, sized to be read across a tourist-information rack from two metres away. No prices, no timetable, no clutter — the cover’s only job is to make a hand reach out and open the fold. Unfold once and the layout changes its manners entirely: now the copy is chatty, benefit-led, and organised into scannable chunks under bold subheadings — “A Day For All Ages,” “Dining On The Move,” “Bring The Dog.” Notice that every heading promises an experience rather than describing a service: the brochure sells the afternoon, not the train. Bullet points and small icons carry the practical detail — a steam icon, a pushchair icon, a wheelchair icon — so information is absorbed at a glance rather than read as prose. A pull-quote in a decorative typeface floats across the centre fold: “The children didn’t look at a single screen all day.” — Mrs P., Harrogate, borrowing the ethos of an ordinary satisfied visitor rather than the company’s own voice. The colour and typography stay ruthlessly consistent — the same racing green, the same heritage serif — so the whole object feels like a small extension of the railway itself. The reading path is engineered by the folds: desire on the cover, reasons on the inside, and — on the final panel, where the eye lands last — conversion. Here, and only here, are the timetable, the ticket prices, the map, the “How to find us,” and a QR code stamped with “Book online and save 10%.” The structure is a funnel: the fold that opened out of curiosity closes on a call to action, having spent three panels earning the right to ask for money.

07 Cartoon (editorial / political) Single-panel satirical drawing commenting on current affairs.exemplar +
Reading the image
  • Caricature — exaggerated features signal identity and judgement
  • Symbols and labels let the reader decode figures and issues
  • Scale and proportion encode power (who towers, who shrinks)
Argument & irony
  • A single, distilled verdict on an event
  • Irony via juxtaposition of the incongruous
  • The caption or speech bubble sharpens the barb
Context & allusion
  • Relies on shared cultural/political knowledge the reader supplies
  • Often parodies a famous image, painting or phrase
  • Timely — anchored to a specific news moment
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Identify the target and the cartoonist's stance toward it
  • Decode every label and symbol before analysing effect
  • Explain the allusion — the humour lives in the gap the reader fills
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

An annotated reading of a single-panel editorial cartoon published during a climate summit.

The panel needs no more than a glance to land its argument, which is the whole discipline of the form. A vast, comfortable figure sprawls in a deckchair, labelled across his belly “FOSSIL LOBBY,” sunglasses on, drink in hand. At his feet, drawn a tenth of his size, a tiny delegate in a suit waters a single wilting sapling marked “PLEDGES.” The scale contrast is not decoration — it is the entire thesis: real power lounges and sunbathes while token gestures are performed, earnestly and uselessly, in its shadow. Caricature does the identifying work — the lobbyist is all soft bulk and leisure, the delegate all anxious diligence — so the reader assigns sympathy and contempt before reading a single word. Behind them, a factory chimney has been drawn as a melting candle, a visual pun that collapses two ideas into one image: industry, and time running out. The symbolism is legible precisely because it relies on shared cultural knowledge the reader supplies — we already know what a summit is, what “pledges” tend to be worth, whose interests tend to win. The caption underneath is a single dry line: “Relax — we’ve set an ambitious target for 2100.” The irony lives in the gap between the reassuring words and the sunbathing giant above them: the reader is trusted to feel the outrage the drawing itself withholds, which is why the cartoon is far more effective than any caption that simply announced its anger. Note also what is absent — there is no earth, no future generation, no consequence shown; the frame is deliberately narrow, and that narrowness is an argument too, mimicking the tunnel vision it criticises. In one still image the cartoonist has delivered a verdict — that the machinery of concern is a performance staged for, and controlled by, the very interests it claims to restrain — with an economy no editorial column could match.

08 Comic strip / Graphic novel extract Sequential art telling a story across panels.exemplar +
Structure & sequence
  • Panels and gutters control the passage of time
  • The gutter is where the reader infers the unshown action (closure)
  • Panel size and shape pace the moment — a wide panel slows time
Word & image
  • Speech balloons vs thought balloons vs narrative captions
  • Onomatopoeia and lettering render sound and volume
  • Meaning lives in the interplay of image and text
Visual grammar
  • Framing, angle and shot size direct emphasis and power
  • Colour palette carries mood; a shift signals a tonal turn
  • Motion lines and posture convey energy and emotion
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Analyse the gutter — what does the reader fill in?
  • Read camera angle as characterisation (low = power)
  • Track colour/lettering shifts as structural signals
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

An annotated reading of a four-panel sequence from a graphic novel, in which a boy tells his mother he is leaving home.

The sequence works through what it shows and, more powerfully, through what it leaves in the gaps between panels. Panel one is wide and almost empty — a kitchen table, mother and son at opposite ends, a great trench of white tablecloth between them. The framing itself states the distance the dialogue will only confirm. His speech balloon is small and low: “I’ve taken the job. In Berlin.” Her reply occupies a thought balloon, not a spoken one — “after everything” — meaning she says nothing at all, and the reader is handed the difference between what is felt and what is voiced. Panel two tightens to a close-up of her hands folding a napkin that does not need folding; the “camera” has chosen her hands over her face, and the displacement of feeling onto a small, controlled gesture does more than any drawn expression could. Panel three is the gutter’s masterpiece: we do not see her stand or cross the room. Between panel two and panel four, in the white space of the gutter, the reader performs the action themselves — this is closure, the defining grammar of comics, where meaning is made in the space the artist refuses to draw. Panel four, finally, is a low angle looking up: she is embracing him, and the upward camera angle briefly restores her power and stature in the very moment the story seemed to strip it away. The colour has drained across the four frames — warm ochre in panel one, near-monochrome by panel four — a palette shift doing the emotional work of a paragraph. There is a single onomatopoeic word, “tik,” the kitchen clock, lettered small in a corner of every panel — a visual metronome counting out a silence too large for speech. The extract never states its subject, and does not need to: the panels, the gutters, and the drained colour have already said everything.

09 Diary / Journal entry Private, dated first-person record of events and feeling.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Dated entries, addressed to self or 'Dear Diary'
  • Associative, fragmentary — thought as it actually arrives
  • No neat resolution; the entry stops rather than ends
Tone, register & voice
  • Confessional, unguarded intimacy — the source of its honesty
  • Present-tense immediacy and raw, unedited reaction
  • No audience awareness (or a pretended one)
Language & diction
  • Ellipsis and dashes mimicking real, unfinished thinking
  • Exclamation, capitalisation and underlining for feeling
  • Private references and shorthand only the writer needs
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • The lack of an audience is the convention — analyse the intimacy
  • Punctuation is characterisation: dashes = a racing mind
  • Note the gap between what's said and what's clearly felt
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

A private diary entry written late at night by a seventeen-year-old the evening before her final exam results.

11:52 p.m. Can’t sleep. Obviously. The clipped, unpunctuated opening is the convention doing its work — there is no audience to perform for, so the sentences arrive half-dressed, the way real thought does. Tomorrow the envelope decides everything and I keep — no. I’m not going to spiral tonight. I’m NOT. The capitalisation isn’t emphasis for a reader; it’s a girl arguing with herself on the page, and losing. The thing is I already know how I did, I can feel it in my teeth, it’s that Paper 2 question about power, I read it and my mind just — went white. I wrote something. I genuinely don’t remember what. The dashes and the trailing “just —” aren’t sloppy; they’re a mind interrupting itself, the punctuation of panic. Mum keeps saying “whatever happens, we’re proud,” in that soft voice she uses for injured animals, and I want to scream that being proud of me is not the same as it being okay. The entry keeps circling back without resolving, which is exactly what a diary does and an essay never would — it has no thesis, no conclusion, only the shape of a feeling being turned over and over. If it’s a 6 I will — I don’t even know how to finish that sentence, which is probably the truest thing I’ve written all night. It’s just a number. It’s just a number. It’s just a number. Writing it three times doesn’t make it true; if anything the repetition proves how much it isn’t. Dad thinks I’m asleep. I can hear him not-sleeping too, the stairs did that creak. Funny — I’m more scared for him than for me. Going to try to sleep now. I won’t. The entry simply stops rather than ends, the way the night itself refused to.

10 Editorial The newspaper's unsigned, institutional opinion piece.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • States the issue, concedes the other side, then plants a firm thesis
  • Marshals evidence and precedent before the verdict
  • Ends on a call for action, policy or reflection
Tone, register & voice
  • Speaks as 'we' — the paper's collective institutional voice
  • Formal but urgent; authoritative without a byline
  • Measured indignation rather than personal rant
Language & diction
  • Balanced concessive constructions ('While X, nevertheless Y')
  • Rhetorical questions and controlled emotive diction
  • Modality signals the paper's certainty ('must', 'cannot')
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • The 'we' is a rhetorical construct — analyse its authority effect
  • Track the concession-then-rebuttal move; it is the whole design
  • Modal verbs carry the argument's force
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

A national newspaper's unsigned editorial responding to a proposed mass-surveillance bill.

No one disputes that a government’s first duty is to keep its citizens safe. The editorial opens, as editorials do, by conceding the strongest point of the other side — a show of fairness that earns the paper the authority to disagree. But safety purchased with privacy is a currency that, once spent, is never refunded. The extended metaphor of currency runs quietly through the whole piece, converting an abstract civil-liberties argument into something a reader can weigh in the hand. The Bill before Parliament this week would grant the security services the power to monitor communications without a warrant — a power that every government in history has promised to use sparingly, and that no government in history has. Notice the collective “we” that follows: “We have seen where this road leads. We have read that history, and we do not propose to reread it.” The “we” is a rhetorical construction — the paper speaking as the settled conscience of the nation, its authority precisely the fact that no single name signs it. The register is formal but charged with a controlled, institutional indignation, never a personal rant: modal verbs carry the force — “Parliament must amend this Bill. It cannot, in conscience, pass it as written.” A rhetorical question sharpens the middle of the argument — “Must we become a society that watches everyone in order to catch someone?” — inviting the reader to answer, silently and in agreement, before the paper answers for them. The structure is a deliberate arc: concession, evidence, precedent, and only then the verdict. The closing sentence reaches for the memorable: “A free press does not ask its readers to fear the dark. It asks only that they refuse to let anyone switch off the lights.” The metaphor of currency has become a metaphor of light, but the move is the same throughout — an institution converting a complex policy into a plain moral choice, and placing itself, and its readers, on the right side of it.

11 Email / Electronic correspondence Digital message whose register varies sharply by recipient.exemplar +
Structure & organisation
  • Subject line, salutation, body, sign-off — all conventional cues
  • Purpose stated in the opening line; concise, single-topic body
  • Threading, forwarding and cc culture implied
Register & tone
  • Formal mode: politeness formulae, hedging, full sentences
  • Casual mode: contractions, abbreviations, emoji, ellipsis
  • Register shifts with hierarchy and relationship
Language & diction
  • Hedging softens requests ('I was wondering whether...')
  • Discourse markers organise multi-point messages
  • Sign-off encodes the relationship ('Best' vs 'Cheers' vs 'Regards')
Examiner tells (convention to marks)
  • Read the salutation and sign-off as relationship data
  • Measure formality against the power gap between sender and recipient
  • Hedging = politeness strategy; name it
Annotated exemplar · ~300 words · highlights mark the convention at work

A formal email from a junior employee to a senior manager requesting a deadline extension, shown with the conventions that manage its register.

Subject: Request for short extension — Henderson report. The subject line does the email’s first job before a word of the body is read: it names the purpose and the project, so a busy recipient can triage it in a second. “Dear Ms Achebe,” — the full formal salutation immediately encodes the power gap between a junior writer and a senior reader; there is no “Hi,” and certainly no first name. The purpose is stated in the opening sentence, not buried: “I am writing to ask whether it might be possible to extend the deadline for the Henderson report by two working days.” The heavy hedging — “whether it might be possible,” “I would be grateful,” “if it is not too much trouble” — is the politeness strategy the whole email runs on, softening what is, underneath, a request that carries a cost to the reader. Sample opening — memorise the shape, not the words

“The most dangerous thing a system can do to a rebel is not silence them — it is sell their rebellion back to them at a profit. Decades later, Banksy's Girl with Balloon shreds itself at auction in protest against the art market — and the market simply renames the wreckage and doubles the price. The 5-minute Q&A — likely questions & your angles

Why is this a stronger reading than “surveillance”?Surveillance is about power watching; co-option is about power digesting — a subtler mechanism that both works dramatise, and one that explains why rebellion in each ultimately fails or feeds the system.
How does form shape meaning differently?Orwell uses the interrogation's dialogue and reversal to enact absorption in language; Banksy uses a live performance inside the auction house, so the event and its site carry the meaning the image alone cannot.
Isn't Banksy just a hypocrite, then?Concede the tension — then argue the self-shredding knows it: the work is a deliberate commentary on its own commodification, which is more honest than pretending to sit outside the market.
Where do the works diverge?Orwell's co-option is imposed by a state that fabricates dissent; Banksy's is imposed by a market that buys it. One is authoritarian, one is capitalist — same paradox, different structure.
Is genuine resistance possible in either?Orwell locates a flicker in the proles and in “2+2=4”; Banksy in the act of naming the trap. Both suggest resistance survives only where the structure cannot yet price it.

The guiding question is a gift

You must genuinely answer it, but you may (and should) go beyond it. An analysis that ignores the guiding question cannot reach the top bands of Criterion C.

The reading protocol — first 10–12 minutes, no writing

  1. TAP-P. Nail Text type, Audience, Purpose, Publication / context, then pull the matching convention checklist from Section I.
  2. Answer the guiding question first in a rough sentence — that becomes your thesis's core.
  3. Annotate for the big three: diction/tone, structure/layout, and visual/multimodal features. Mark 6–8 quotable moments.
  4. Find the shift. Every text turns — tone, register, address, or focus. The turn is usually your strongest paragraph.

Thesis formula

Through [2–3 named techniques], [text] constructs [tone/stance] in order to [purpose] for [specific audience] — a strategy complicated / sharpened by [a tension or shift].

The final clause is what separates a 4 from a 5: never write a thesis a summary could reach.

9–10Excellent — perceptive, sustained, sophisticated; a genuine, insightful comparison.

The comparison must be genuine

The commonest 5–6 band trap is two essays stapled together (“In Work A… In Work B…”). Examiners reward integrated comparison: every body paragraph holds both works inside the same idea, moving on connectives — similarly, conversely, whereas, by contrast, likewise, where A…, B instead….

Choosing the question & the works

Pick the question your two works answer differently but richly. The best essays exploit divergence: same theme, opposite treatment. Underline the command term (how / to what extent / in what ways) and the focus (character, setting, structure, technique), then convert the question into a thesis in your first two sentences.

Thesis formula

While both [A] and [B] explore [theme], [A] presents it as [X] through [technique], whereas [B] frames it as [Y] through [technique] — a divergence rooted in [context / form / purpose].

Structure options

1Point-by-point (best). Each paragraph = one idea, both works woven in. Highest-scoring, hardest to fake.
2Thematic threads. 3–4 threads (e.g. power, resistance, cost), each comparing the works.
3Technique-led. Compare via craft — narrative voice, structure, symbolism, form — not plot.

Winning Criterion B

Not what happens but how the authors construct meaning: perspective, structure, motif, form, and the effect on the reader. Marry each claim to a micro-quotation and a technique, and reference authorial choice — “Orwell positions…”, “the novel withholds…” — using the analytical-verb bank in Section VI.

Closed-book quotation strategy

Memorise 8–12 short quotations per work, tagged by theme, plus five structural facts each (how it opens, turns, ends; a key motif; the form). A three-word quotation you can analyse outscores a full sentence you merely reproduce. Build a quotation grid per work in the weeks before the exam and self-test it.

Timing (1h 45m)

~15 min choose the question + plan a comparative skeleton (thesis + 4 integrated points + quotations). ~85 min write. ~5 min check the thesis is answered and both works are balanced.

Common ways students lose marks

  • Two works discussed in isolation, never truly compared (ceiling: Criterion C).
  • Narrating the plot instead of analysing authorial choices (ceiling: Criterion B).
  • Not answering the actual question — writing everything they know instead.
  • Unbalanced essays that run out of time on Work 2.
  • Vague, half-remembered “quotations” — precision matters more than length.
